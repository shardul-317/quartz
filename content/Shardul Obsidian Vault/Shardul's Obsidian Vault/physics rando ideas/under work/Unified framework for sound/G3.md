### **G3: Shockwave Generation**

**Objective**: Model shockwave formation and propagation using the Burgers' equation and analyze their acoustic implications.

---

##### **3.3.1 Derivation of Burgers' Equation**

1. **Inviscid Burgers' Equation**:  
   Starting with the 1D momentum equation from the Navier-Stokes equations, neglecting viscosity:  
   $$
   \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + v \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = 0.
$$  
   This is the **inviscid Burgers' equation**, capturing the nonlinear steepening of waves.

2. **Nonlinear Steepening**:  
   The term $v \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$ causes wave profiles to steepen. For a initially smooth velocity profile $v(x, 0) = f(x)$, the solution can be found using the method of characteristics:  
   $$
   v(x, t) = f(x - v t).
$$  
   This leads to the formation of a shock wave at time $t_s = \frac{1}{\max|\partial f/\partial x|}$.

3. **Viscous Burgers' Equation**:  
   Adding viscosity to regularize the shock:  
   $$
   \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + v \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x^2},
$$  
   where $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity. This equation balances nonlinear steepening with viscous diffusion.

---

##### **3.3.2 Shock Formation and N-Wave Solution**

1. **Shock Formation Time**:  
   The time to form a shock is given by:  
   $$
   t_s = \frac{1}{\max\left(\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right|\right)_{t=0}}.
$$  
   After $t_s$, the solution develops a discontinuity (shock).

2. **N-Wave Solution**:  
   The **N-wave** is a typical shockwave profile resulting from the viscous Burgers' equation. The solution is:  
   $$
   v(x, t) = \frac{2\nu}{\sqrt{\pi \nu t}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - U t)^2}{4\nu t}\right) \text{erfc}\left(\frac{x - U t}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right),
$$  
   where $U$ is the shock speed and $\text{erfc}$ is the complementary error function.

---

##### **3.3.3 Acoustic Implications**

1. **Pressure Profile**:  
   The pressure jump across a shock wave is related to the velocity discontinuity. For a normal shock:  
   $$
   \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\rho_0 c_0} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{v_1 + v_2}{c_0} \right)^2 \left( \frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma - 1} \right),
$$  
   where $v_1$ and $v_2$ are the velocities upstream and downstream of the shock, respectively.

2. **Radiated Acoustic Waves**:  
   The pressure fluctuations from shockwaves propagate as acoustic waves. For a moving shock (e.g., in a jet engine), the radiated pressure can be modeled using the **Ffowcs Williams-Hawkings equation** with appropriate source terms.

---

##### **3.3.4 Dimensional Analysis**

1. **Burgers' Equation**:  
   - **Nonlinear Term**: $[v \partial v/\partial x] = \text{m/s} \cdot \text{s}^{-1} = \text{s}^{-2}$.  
   - **Viscous Term**: $[\nu \partial^2 v/\partial x^2] = \text{m}^2/\text{s} \cdot \text{s}^{-1}/\text{m}^2 = \text{s}^{-2}$.  

2. **Pressure Jump**:  
   - **Shock Relation**: $[p] = \text{Pa} = \text{kg}/(\text{m} \cdot \text{s}^2)$.  

---

##### **3.3.5 Case Study: Sonic Boom**

**Problem**: Model the pressure signature of a sonic boom from a supersonic aircraft.

1. **Shockwave Angle**:  
   The Mach angle $\mu$ is given by:  
   $$
   \sin \mu = \frac{1}{M},
$$  
   where $M$ is the Mach number.

2. **Pressure Signature**:  
   The N-wave profile is characteristic of sonic booms. The peak overpressure $\Delta p$ can be estimated using:  
   $$
   \Delta p \approx \rho_0 c_0^2 \left( \frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma - 1} \right) \left( \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2c_0} \right)^2.
$$

3. **Example Calculation**:  
   For an aircraft flying at $M = 2$ with $v_1 = 300 \, \text{m/s}$ and $v_2 = 100 \, \text{m/s}$:  
   $$
   \Delta p \approx 1.225 \times (343)^2 \times \frac{4}{0.4} \times \left( \frac{200}{686} \right)^2 \approx 1.225 \times 117649 \times 10 \times 0.184 \approx 26,500 \, \text{Pa}.
$$

---

##### **3.3.6 Limitations**

1. **1D Approximation**:  
   Burgers' equation is inherently 1D. Real shocks are often 3D and require more complex modeling.

2. **Newtonian Viscosity**:  
   The viscous term assumes Newtonian fluid behavior, which may not hold in rarified flows (see ).

3. **Weak Shock Assumption**:  
   The acoustic radiation model assumes weak shocks. Strong shocks may require Rankine-Hugoniot relations.
